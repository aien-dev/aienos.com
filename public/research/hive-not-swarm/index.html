Hive, not swarm

Control, flow and collapse in human-directed coding-agent teams.

Drake Stapleton · 29 October 2026
Revision 3 18 pages Preprint · Not peer reviewed
Read PDF DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23766142 Zenodo record Source repo Cite

Abstract

Multi-agent coding systems are often called swarms. I argue that the arrangement that delivers accountable software is structurally different, and I call it a hive. In a swarm, agents choose their own work from local cues, coordinate through a shared medium, and the result emerges from their interactions. In a hive, every agent holds an assignment, writes only inside an owned lane, reads lateral messages as data rather than commands, and one human holds acceptance and stopping authority.

Drawing on swarm intelligence, social-insect biology, supervisory control and manufacturing control, the article defines the two structures on four axes (who selects work, what lateral signals mean, who may write shared state, and how work ends) and identifies when central direction is feasible: while an informed supervisor and a system-wide channel exist. It then examines a dated operational record from the author's AIEN agent hive, 13 to 28 October 2026, in which the hive repeatedly degraded into swarm-like behavior. Seven mechanisms recur: overlapping authority, coupling through shared derived state, tool drift, channel fragmentation, supervisor saturation or loss, exclusive-resource breaches and unreconciled duplicate work. Every recorded recovery followed the same sequence: stop, one owner per resource, re-issued lanes and a reconciled ledger. A constrained concurrency model shows how review capacity bounds useful concurrency.

The record is retrospective, self-reported and uncontrolled. It supports a mechanism taxonomy and preregisterable hypotheses, including a fault-injection design, not causal claims. Not peer reviewed. The source bundle contains the article builder, figures, model verifier, repository counts and a hashed extract of the operational record.
